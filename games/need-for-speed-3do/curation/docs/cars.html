Twenty-nine car families ship under DriveData/CarData for eight playable cars — the rest are the traffic fleet, the police Mustang, and a shelf of vehicles nothing in the game ever spawns. A family file is a wwww wrap container holding (model, texture-set) pairs per detail level plus the physics tunables; the highest-detail Diablo is 61 textured quads over 102 vertices, and one model unit is 1/128 m — the Diablo's ±292-unit length works out to 4.56 m, the real car to the centimetre.

ORI3: everything is a quad

An ORI3 model is two flat arrays: vertices as integer XYZ triples and 24-byte face records — a material word, an id, four vertex indices. Every face is a quad, because the Madam cel engine draws quads: a textured face is one cel mapped corner-to-corner. Triangles are quads with the fourth index repeated. The material word's top byte is a face-class tag (plain body, degenerate triangle, or the late-drawn wheel overlays); the texture binding lives outside the model, in the paired shape's !ori table — one {face, texture} entry per quad.

SPoT textures and the shading in the pixels

The textures are tiny — 4×8 to 32×32 — in the shape's SPoT records: palette-coded pixels at 1 to 8 bits, optionally run-length packed per line, each record pointing at its palette chunk by a signed 24-bit offset. The 8-bit types put a 5-bit palette index under a 3-bit brightness multiplier in each pixel: the cel engine scales every channel by it, which is where the window reflections and panel gradients live — shading painted into the pixels of a 32-colour texture.

Four liveries, one palette pointer

A traffic-car shape carries four shared palette chunks, plt0–plt3: entries 0–13 hold the chrome, glass and tyre colours, identical in all four; entries 14+ are the body ramp — teal, red, blue, white for the BMW. Every texture points at plt0; when a traffic car spawns, the engine simply retargets the cel's palette pointer at one of the four chunks, chosen by the same linear-congruential roll that places the car. Four liveries for the cost of 80 bytes each — the Studio's variant picker shows the same four schemes.

Drawing without a depth buffer

The renderer paints a car's quads in model order with no depth buffer — later faces simply cover earlier ones. The models are built for it: the silver wheel quads sit inboard of the body panels (the Diablo's rear rims a couple of centimetres inside its sills), the wheel arches are painted black in the body texture as a backdrop, and the wheels draw late, over the top. Behind each rim hangs a near-black disc — the far wheel you see through a wheel well is that disc, not the other rim's back. A depth-tested viewer inverts the trick — the outboard black arch wins and eats the wheel — so the Studio's export nudges each bright rim quad just outside the body surface that overlaps it, restoring the painted-over look from every angle.

Bilinear quads and the straight-line problem

The cel engine maps a texture onto a quad bilinearly — its corner matrix walks texture rows and columns with a per-row shear, linear in both coordinates together. On a trapezoid quad (the Rodeo's cabin side tapers a full metre front to back) no pair of affinely-textured triangles reproduces that mapping: straight painted lines like window pillars kink at the diagonal. The Studio's GLB export subdivides each non-parallelogram quad into a grid until it converges on the cel engine's mapping — which is why the viewer's triangle count dwarfs the native quad count in the stats panel.

The shelf of the unspawned

The disc carries vehicles the game never uses: a green scooter with a helmeted rider, fully textured; a classic-body 911 at higher polygon count than the retail 911; a development iteration of the police Mustang; and a Probe94 family that actually contains the SASCO000 van — a mislabeled file sitting next to the correctly named copy. The 512 TR's internal model name is TESTA000: its body is a Testarossa. Several opponent-art files are byte-identical copies of the Viper's — placeholders that shipped.
