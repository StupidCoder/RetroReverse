The sixty-three files under /Cars hold every vehicle in the game: fifteen driveable Ferraris, their fifteen _t twins, fifteen lower-poly AI rivals, the traffic fleet and a bundle of every rival in one file. Each is a bare zlib stream — .sz has no container of its own — that the game inflates through two 64 KB ping-pong buffers, so a whole car never needs to exist decompressed in one piece anywhere except video memory.

The _pmt model format

An inflated _pmt payload is a 16-byte header and two sections: section A carries the CPU-side tables, section B the GPU data — texture pixels first, vertex buffers after. At load, a fix-up pass walks the part records and turns every nonzero offset into a pointer; which fields it touches is what defines the format.

Parts, batches, materials

A car is a list of parts — body, doors, wheels, steering wheel — each a 15-word record pointing at its index-buffer slices, vertex-buffer pairs, draw batches and materials. Every batch names a primitive run in the part's slice of a shared 16-bit index pool, a base vertex, and a 0x58-byte material whose four stages carry texture indices into the file's XPR0 texture bank. Vertices are compact: a position, a normal packed into one 11:11:10 word, and (on textured parts) one UV pair — 16 or 24 bytes each. Textures are DXT-compressed sheets; the bank also carries each car's static environment cube, six faces in a row.

Material state in one word

Each material carries a packed state key. Its second byte indexes the game's own blend factor table twice — source and destination — and three more bits pick the blend equation; other fields carry a depth-bias step for decal overlays and winding control for mirrored geometry. The window glass reads ONE / 1−SRC_ALPHA (premultiplied alpha, tinted by the material's diffuse alpha of 0.55), and the light glows read SRC_ALPHA / ONE — genuine additive blending, straight from the file. The Studio's exports reproduce exactly these modes.

Every part in local space

The model files hold every articulated part at the origin: wheels, doors, the steering wheel and gear stick all sit centred, and nothing in the file says where they belong. The placements live in a chassis table inside the executable: per car, the exact translation of each wheel (real wheelbases to the centimetre — the Dino's 2.34 m, the Enzo's 2.65 m), the door hinges, the gear stick, and the steering wheel with its column tilt in radians. The player cars stack up to four parts per wheel corner — tire, rim, disc — while one car's record has an empty gear-stick slot because its lever is modelled into the interior. The Studio assembles every car from this table.

Levels of detail

Each rival carries five versions of itself. The near model is a full group — body, front detail, separate spinning wheels — and four progressively simpler bodies take over with distance, most with their wheels baked in place. The farthest is a shell of a few dozen triangles with no wheels at all: at that range it only ever covers a handful of pixels. One car's mid model genuinely has no wheels anywhere — the game accepts a floating body at that distance. The Studio's variant picker shows each level as the game holds it.

The shadow caster that is never seen

One part of every rival is a closed grey hull, roughly the car up to its beltline. It never appears on screen: it is drawn only from the light's point of view, into the shadow map — a cheap silhouette standing in for the real car, so shadows cost a few hundred triangles instead of thousands. The Studio shows it as its own variant.

Liveries: textures riding on quads that never draw

The AI cars come in up to seven paint schemes, shipped as whole-car texture atlases in different colours. The format has no way to store a texture on its own — a texture exists only if a material references it, a material only exists on a batch, and a batch needs vertices. So each livery atlas is attached to a tiny unit quad, and the quads sit in a neat stack inside an effects part that the game never submits for drawing. At race time the engine simply binds the chosen livery atlas wherever a material names the body-texture slot — a per-instance texture substitution. The same trick swaps the file's static environment cube for a live-rendered reflection cube while driving; at rest, the static cube is the paint's sheen, and the Studio applies it the same way.
