Ordinary barrier hits are handled inside the continuous model, not by a canned state: at an instrumented 116 km/h berm impact the lateral position pins at the wall, speed scrubs from 32 to 9 m/s over a second and a half, the body yaws thirty degrees and the slip angle spikes — tyre smoke and all — with not one flag changing in the car struct. Below the crash thresholds a glancing blow is just a kick: a shove on the yaw rate, clamped, scaled by how square-on you hit.

The spin and the rollover

Above the thresholds the game swaps the car's per-tick behaviour function — the car struct carries a handler pointer, and the crash entry replaces the driving model with a crash simulation. Two entries, one simulation. The spin arms when the car slides sideways faster than 22 m/s (about 50 mph laterally; the bar rises to 32 m/s on some surfaces), gets a two-second budget and mostly yaw. The rollover demands a real wall: an impact component of at least 16 m/s and the car above 32 m/s — 72 mph — of speed. Its budget is thirteen seconds, enough for the tumble, the rest, and the tow to the respawn; it also raises the crashed flag for the HUD and cues the crash sound.

Neither animation is a canned clip. The entry transforms the impact velocity through the car's own 3×3 orientation matrix into body frame — those are the tumble's initial conditions — and the angle of incidence, folded into a quarter circle, blends how much of the energy becomes yaw spin versus end-over-end roll: clip a wall shallow and you pirouette, hit it square at speed and you go over the roof. Each tick the handler then runs a stripped rigid body: no wheels, no suspension, just the orientation matrix spun by the seeded angular velocity, the position falling ballistically on the same 1/30 s fixed-point integrator the driving model uses, and a ground-plane bounce whose restitution comes from the track segment's surface bytes. The severity is tempered — six metres per second come off the impact before it seeds the tumble, and what remains is clamped — so every rollover reads as violent but stays on camera.

Four cars per season

The CRASHED! CARS LEFT banner is backed by real bookkeeping: a season-level crash count kept by the front-end, and an in-race spares counter seeded with three from the race configuration — four cars in all. A rollover consumes a car only when the contact carries the chargeable-surface bit; the crash state machine actually decrements at two hand-off points and refunds one when the crash wasn't chargeable. The banner samples its digit before the bookkeeping settles, which is why your first crash of a session always reads CARS LEFT: 3. What happens when the stock runs dry is decided between races by the front-end, not by the race engine — that judgement is the one piece of the crash system still untraced.
