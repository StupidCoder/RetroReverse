Everything that moves on the road — the player, the rival, the traffic, the cop — lives in one pool of seven car structs, each carrying a driver function pointer as its brain. Three brains exist: the player's control path, one shared road AI, and a dormant-pool driver that counts down spawn gates and then rewrites its own car's driver pointer to the road AI. What makes a cop a cop and traffic traffic is not code but a per-car behaviour bitmask selecting which AI modules run: the pace governor, the cop module, lane steering, an oncoming-direction bit.

Two motion models on one road

Only the player is simulated. The player's car integrates forces into body-frame velocities through a full rigid-body model — orientation matrix, slip angle (the tyre smoke reads it), grip state, a 100-sample power curve and per-gear thresholds from the plain-text tunables. Every AI car — rival, cops, traffic alike — is kinematic: rails with a spring. Its position advances along a velocity vector, then a corrective spring pulls it toward a road anchor point; no forces, no slip, no orientation dynamics. The difficulty system rides the same split: per-skill Handling and Speed factors in the text files scale how much curves slow an AI car and what fraction of the reference top-speed line it may hold — the generic traffic class pins handling at 255, never slowed by a curve.

Eight lanes, scored

The steering module quantises the 40-metre road into eight five-metre lanes and scores each car's current lane against its neighbours: penalties keep same-way cars right of centre and oncoming cars left, the segment's own bytes narrow the drivable range, and an eight-slot occupancy map built from the other six cars feeds avoidance. The winning lane's centreline becomes the steering target. Oncoming traffic is the same code with one bit set — and its road-frame motion block negated wholesale.

A traffic system of seven cars

Traffic exists only in a window from 120 m behind the player to 324 m ahead; a car drifting outside re-enters the dormant pool and respawns ahead. Placement rolls an inline linear-congruential generator for a 50/50 direction coin (overridden in one-way zones), caps each direction at three active cars, and rolls the same stream again for the model and its livery — the four shared palette schemes each class carries. A handful of structs, recycled forever, reads as a living highway.

The cop's day

The cop idles pre-armed until the player comes within two segments at speed; that sets a pursuit flag on the player's mask. The bust compares a warning counter against a threshold from the constants files: under it a written warning, over it a speeding ticket — two different overlay cel families — while a pull-over choreography parks both cars at the roadside before the chase flags clear and the race resumes.

Crashes without scripts

Ordinary barrier hits are handled inside the continuous model, not by a canned state: at an instrumented 116 km/h berm impact the lateral position pins at the wall, speed scrubs from 32 to 9 m/s over a second and a half, the body yaws thirty degrees and the slip angle spikes — tyre smoke and all — with not one flag changing in the car struct. Below the crash thresholds a glancing blow is just a kick: a shove on the yaw rate, clamped, scaled by how square-on you hit.

The spin and the rollover

Above the thresholds the game swaps the car's per-tick behaviour function — the car struct carries a handler pointer, and the crash entry replaces the driving model with a crash simulation. Two entries, one simulation. The spin arms when the car slides sideways faster than 22 m/s (about 50 mph laterally; the bar rises to 32 m/s on some surfaces), gets a two-second budget and mostly yaw. The rollover demands a real wall: an impact component of at least 16 m/s and the car above 32 m/s — 72 mph — of speed. Its budget is thirteen seconds, enough for the tumble, the rest, and the tow to the respawn; it also raises the crashed flag for the HUD and cues the crash sound.

Neither animation is a canned clip. The entry transforms the impact velocity through the car's own 3×3 orientation matrix into body frame — those are the tumble's initial conditions — and the angle of incidence, folded into a quarter circle, blends how much of the energy becomes yaw spin versus end-over-end roll: clip a wall shallow and you pirouette, hit it square at speed and you go over the roof. Each tick the handler then runs a stripped rigid body: no wheels, no suspension, just the orientation matrix spun by the seeded angular velocity, the position falling ballistically on the same 1/30 s fixed-point integrator the driving model uses, and a ground-plane bounce whose restitution comes from the track segment's surface bytes. The severity is tempered — six metres per second come off the impact before it seeds the tumble, and what remains is clamped — so every rollover reads as violent but stays on camera.

Four cars per season

The CRASHED! CARS LEFT banner is backed by real bookkeeping: a season-level crash count kept by the front-end, and an in-race spares counter seeded with three from the race configuration — four cars in all. A rollover consumes a car only when the contact carries the chargeable-surface bit; the crash state machine actually decrements at two hand-off points and refunds one when the crash wasn't chargeable. The banner samples its digit before the bookkeeping settles, which is why your first crash of a session always reads CARS LEFT: 3. What happens when the stock runs dry is decided between races by the front-end, not by the race engine — that judgement is the one piece of the crash system still untraced.
