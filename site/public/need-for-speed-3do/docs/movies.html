The disc carries 165 streamed movies — more files than the rest of the disc put together. Each Movies/*.Stream is a flat chunk stream for the SDK's DataStreamer: FILM video subchunks interleaved with SNDS audio. The video is Cinepak — the 3DO has no video-decode hardware, so the ARM60 unpacks every frame in software — and the audio is SDX2, a one-byte-per-sample DPCM the DSP undoes at 22 kHz stereo. The three boot films run at 320×240; everything else is 320×192.

Every movie on the disc plays at 15 frames per second — even though 150 of the 165 declare 30 in their film header. The header field lies; the truth is in the frame timestamps, which tick on the same 240 Hz audio clock that paces the whole engine, sixteen ticks a frame. It has to be 15: a stream fed at 30 fps would need nearly 600 KB/s, and the console's double-speed drive delivers 300. The real rates sit at 190–295 KB/s — the movies are cut to ride just under the drive's ceiling.

What's on the reel rack

Eighteen movies have names: the Electronic Arts and Pioneer logo films, the Road & Track title montage, three attract reels, three trooper pull-over films, the victory montage, and one showcase film for each of the eight player cars. At boot the front-end queues eac, pioneer, one attract reel (a rotation through all three), then title — and the player abandons the whole list the moment one file fails to open, the same graceful fallback that lets a scratched disc skip straight to the menus.

The other ~147 are numbered N.M reels: the host's post-race commentary, live-action clips composited into tilted magazine pages. N is a topic — an outcome the front-end wants remarked upon — and M one of a few takes of it. After a race the code walks your statistics, picks the topic the numbers call for, and rolls a die for the take: sprintf("N.%d", rand(k)+base), forty-odd such sites. The same dispatchers pick the host's spoken one-liners from parallel tables of audio clips, and the attract mode's magazine segment is the same machinery hardwired to issue 101.

The dispatch itself decodes cleanly. The race dispatcher compares your run against the DriveHS records block (the "Daredevil" defaults shipped in FrontEnd/InitHS) and against the opponent, with thresholds in round numbers of its 60 Hz clock: beat the course record by over two seconds and the host raves; win by five and he bows; lose by fifteen and he winces. Reach 89.3 m/s — 199.8 mph — and the 200-mph reel plays; average under sixty and you get the Sunday-drive routine. A second dispatcher wraps the season against course-total records and target times (five- and thirty-second margins), the champion gets a reel picked from a per-car table, and an arrest cues the trooper film and then the booking commentary. Every reel in the player carries its decoded trigger in the info panel; a handful of crash-and-traffic counters keep their raw block offsets until their engine-side writers are traced.

The cut movies

Walking every dispatcher gives the complete set of reels the shipped code can ever request: 132 of them, every one present on the disc. Which leaves 33 movies that nothing requests — eight whole topic families (35–40, 42 and 51) plus a dozen odd takes, recorded, encoded and pressed onto every copy, unreachable without patching the binary. One of them (38.1) is also the only silent movie on the disc. Commentary for race outcomes that were cut, or trimmed to fit the drive's streaming budget — the movie-side twin of the unused prototype cars parked in the same filesystem. All 33 are in the video player here, under Unused — the same shelf the prototype cars sit on in the model viewer.
