The actors live in two different worlds. The player, the bullets and the enemy helicopter are hardware sprites, updated once per frame from the interrupt chain. The tanks, mines, missiles and prisoners are characters, drawn into the map buffer by engines called from the main loop — and during play that loop never waits for the frame counter: it free-runs, one pass taking about two and a half frames. Every enemy cadence is counted in loop passes, so the character world ticks at its own, slightly slower clock.

The explosion character is the universal bullet

Bullets fly as sprites but on impact stamp the explosion character $20 into the hit map cell. Character actors do not take hits — they find the explosion in their own cell, die, and restore the background they had saved themselves. One mechanism kills mines, tanks, missiles — and prisoners: a stray shot, the player's or the enemy's, can shoot the floor from under the very men to rescue, or the men themselves.

Six tanks, one countdown

The six tanks share a single global step countdown, so they patrol in perfect lockstep. Each is three body cells plus a turret glyph that always aims at the player. Before a step a tank probes the destination against a 22-entry obstacle table that notably includes empty air and the barrier — so tanks reverse at ledges and gates but drive straight through fences, pads and rubble, saving and restoring the background as they pass.

The helicopter that cannot climb

The enemy helicopter is pure per-axis pursuit — one step toward your column, one toward your row, probing the cells ahead so it only advances into clear corridor. It spawns at fixed patrol points, never within 34 columns or 8 rows of the player. But its upward terrain probe is broken by a signature: the hidden ASCII string N0:JOE VIERMON.Z — the porter's name, never displayed — sits in the padding right before the routine, and its final byte lands on the first opcode, turning LDA $76 into EOR $76,X. The column tested while climbing is garbage, so the machine climbs erratically and sometimes clips into ceilings that kill it. A name stored one byte too far is a bug shipped in every copy.

Other curiosities

The title's attract mode flies the helicopter by replaying 108 recorded joystick bytes — a table that reads like text (NNNNKKKKIIII…) because the stick values happen to be letter codes. And the second level's map data contains a graffito drawn in wall characters, off the beaten path: PLEXAR WAS HERE.
