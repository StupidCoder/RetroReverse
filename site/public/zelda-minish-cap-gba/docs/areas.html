Rooms are not islands. Each belongs to an area, and the area's tables say where every one of its rooms sits — as a pixel position on a single shared canvas. That is what makes a walkable place out of a pile of screens: the maps here are whole regions, assembled at the coordinates the game itself uses, not one screen at a time.

The room record

An area's geometry is a list of 10-byte records, one per room, ending in $FFFF. A record is five halfwords: the room's x and y on the area canvas, its width and height, and which of the area's tile sets it draws with — all four positional values in pixels. Dividing a size by 16 gives the metatile grid, because a metatile is 2×2 tiles of eight pixels. The field outside Link's house is 1008×688 pixels — 63×43 metatiles — at (1488, 2480) in its area.

What the engine assembles

On entering a room the engine builds a descriptor from these tables: the size, the position, the three asset lists, and the area's script entry point. The map itself is expanded into a fixed 64×64-metatile buffer — the room's grid copied in at its real width, the remainder cleared — which is why a room can be any size up to that bound without the renderer knowing anything about it.

Five tables, one index

Everything an area owns hangs off its id: the room geometry, the array of per-room asset lists, the array of tile sets its rooms choose between, the metatile tables its rooms share, and the code that runs while the player is there. A room is addressed as an area plus an index within it.

What the areas turn out to be

The grouping is the game's own, and it shows two distinct shapes. The low-numbered areas are large contiguous stretches of overworld: their rooms tile edge to edge into a continuous landscape, seams invisible, a river running from one room into the next. Hyrule Field assembles from ten rooms into 2208×2448 pixels — with a hole in the middle, because Hyrule Castle is not one of its rooms but an area of its own.

The higher-numbered areas are the opposite: a dozen or more small rooms scattered across a mostly empty canvas. These are interiors and dungeon chambers, grouped by the region they belong to rather than joined into one space. Their coordinates keep them apart instead of fitting them together, and the doorways between them are decided by the area's script rather than by adjacency.

Scale

419 rooms resolve through these tables, and they assemble into 79 places.
