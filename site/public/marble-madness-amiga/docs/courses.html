A course is three parallel files. The .mlb holds its look: a tile map 36 tiles (288 pixels) wide, scrolling only vertically, built from 8×8 16-colour tiles. The .ilb holds the art for its objects. And the *Track holds everything else — slopes, respawn points, enemies, scripts, palette bands. The marble never rolls on the tile map at all: the picture and the physics are separate data.

A real height field

The marble is a genuine 3-D point mass — position and velocity in x, y and z, with the isometric look just a projection. The Track's slope records rasterise into a mesh storing the four corner heights of every tile; the downhill force is the sampled gradient of that mesh, and the walls fall out of the same data as height steps between neighbours. One height map produces both the roll and the collision. The Silly course flips a single sign — the slope force is added instead of subtracted — and that one change is the whole joke of the course: the marble accelerates uphill.

Hidden rows

The tile map's header stores the playable height, and the scroll clamp means rows beyond it can never appear on screen — which makes them free storage. Ultimate declares 108 rows but stores 198: the extra 90 are three alternate versions of its final screen. A dedicated engine cycles the four variants — repainting a 30-row band into a back buffer, patching the height mesh so the vanished paths are really gone, and re-pointing the bitplanes mid-frame from the copper list — the flickering finale is a whole screen on a flip-book, at about 1.4 s per page. Silly stores one fully drawn extra wall row that nothing references: authored content the playable height simply excludes.

A tiny bytecode

The animated features run on a 19-opcode script interpreter: keyframes, loops, links to animation lists, marble-proximity conditionals. The drawbridge is an autonomous script looping forever between a raise and a lower animation with timed holds; the goal flags are one opcode playing four wave frames forever. The funnels are the opposite extreme — their scripts are trivial, and the entrance-to-exit teleport is hardcoded in the engine, one handler per terrain code. In all, the game uses eight distinct placement systems for its hazards — slope field, scripted regions, a code-spawned region (Intermediate's wave), an actor array (Aerial's pistons), fall regions with trigger scripts (the vacuums), creature spawners, a lane spawner and the screen-swap engine — almost every hazard family got its own bespoke mechanism.
